Research

Watching single molecules fold, move, and assemble

My work sits at the intersection of biophysics, optics, and statistics — building tools to see what individual proteins and membranes do, one molecule at a time, and interpreting the noisy signals that come with it.

In plain terms

Most of what we know about how proteins fold or assemble comes from measuring the average behavior of billions of molecules at once. But biology is not average — individual proteins can take very different paths to the same destination, and the rare, fast, or minority pathways are often the most important ones. My research develops microscopy and data-analysis methods that let us watch one molecule at a time, so we can catch these hidden behaviors directly instead of guessing at them from bulk averages.

Concretely, I work with single-molecule FRET (a technique that reports on tiny distance changes within or between molecules using fluorescent dyes) combined with plasmonic nanostructures called zero-mode waveguides (ZMWs), which let us watch single molecules at concentrations close to what's found in a real cell — something conventional microscopy struggles with. Because single-molecule data is inherently noisy, I also develop Bayesian inference methods to extract reliable, quantitative kinetic information from these measurements.

Research interests

Current focus
Protein folding transition paths Using plasmonic-enhanced smFRET to directly observe transition paths in protein folding landscapes — the fleeting, hard-to-catch moments between folded and unfolded states.
Methods
Zero-mode waveguides & plasmonics Nanophotonic structures that confine light to observe single molecules at physiologically relevant, high concentrations.
Methods
Bayesian inference for single-molecule data Statistical methods to extract reliable kinetic and conformational information from noisy, photon-limited single-molecule trajectories.
PhD work
Membrane biophysics & pore-forming proteins Assembly dynamics of the pore-forming toxin Cytolysin A (ClyA) on supported lipid bilayers, and membrane crowding by grafted polymers, studied with single-molecule imaging.
