Teaching & Mentoring

I am an experimentalist. I build optical instruments and measure what single molecules do with them, and that is the position I teach from: a result earns belief when a measurement agrees with it.

Teaching statement

A student who has never watched a prediction meet a measurement has been taught to trust authority rather than evidence. Most engineering courses stop at the derivation. The equation is proved, boundary conditions are applied, and the quantity it predicts is never seen. I teach in the other direction wherever the subject allows it, beginning with something measured and reaching the theory as the explanation of it.

The crossing of an activation barrier is my standing example, and it belongs as much to reaction engineering as to my own research. I would open with an observation: an enzyme opens and closes at a rate that falls as the solvent is made more viscous. The Arrhenius expression, as a first course presents it, does not account for that. The derivation then has a purpose, and Kramers' result arrives as the Arrhenius expression with the friction of the surroundings in its prefactor. Only afterwards does a short simulation of a particle in a double well come in, as the cheapest way to see why the expression takes the form it does. The same hour has covered a classical result, a numerical method, and data the class is entitled to argue with.

Computation belongs inside a course rather than beside it, and as an instrument rather than a subject. A graduating chemical engineer should be able to read a piece of analysis code, change it, run it on real data, and defend what they conclude from the output. That is a different skill from writing a program, and it is the one that a research group or an industrial laboratory actually needs.

Assessment should ask for judgement, and should be built so that it cannot be short-circuited. In a large class each student can be issued a different dataset, generated from their roll number by the same simulation code used in lectures, which makes the analysis necessarily their own and keeps marking automatic. In a graduate class, a productive exercise is to write a referee report on a paper whose reviews were published openly, and then read the reviews the paper actually received.

Courses I am prepared to teach

Core courses in a chemical engineering curriculum, which I can take from the beginning of a semester.

Chemical Engineering Mathematics

Undergraduate core. Taught as a teaching assistant at the Indian Institute of Science, August to December 2017.

Linear algebra, ordinary and partial differential equations, numerical methods, probability, and an introduction to stochastic processes.

Transport Phenomena

Undergraduate core, with a graduate extension.

Momentum, heat and mass transfer; diffusion and convection; transport in porous and crowded media; transport at interfaces and in biological systems, where my own measurements lie.

Chemical Engineering Thermodynamics

Undergraduate core, with a graduate extension in statistical thermodynamics.

Classical thermodynamics, phase and reaction equilibria, molecular interactions, and the statistical basis of free energy, carried through to the assembly of biological structures.

Chemical Reaction Engineering

Undergraduate core.

Kinetics, reactor design and analysis, residence-time distributions, catalysis, and enzyme and biochemical reaction networks.

Numerical Methods and Computational Modelling

Undergraduate or first-year graduate.

Numerical solution of engineering problems in Python and MATLAB, stochastic simulation, parameter estimation, and the honest reporting of uncertainty.

Undergraduate laboratory

Undergraduate core.

Any of the standard unit-operations and reaction-engineering experiments, taught with the instrumentation, calibration and error analysis that a laboratory course usually leaves implicit.

Where a department offers a first course in biology or biophysics for engineering students, I am glad to take it. My research sits on that boundary, and I have taught practical microscopy to a mixed class of biologists, physicists and engineers.

Elective courses I would develop

Three graduate electives that follow from my research and that I could teach from my own data and code. Each is built on a piece of core chemical engineering rather than beside it.

Crossing Barriers: rare events from reactors to proteins

Proposed graduate elective. Prerequisites: differential equations, undergraduate thermodynamics and kinetics.

Random walks; the Langevin and Fokker–Planck equations; first-passage times; Kramers' theory in its low- and high-friction limits; transition paths and committors; classical nucleation theory; stochastic simulation by the Gillespie algorithm. Case studies are taken from catalysis, from crystal nucleation, from the conformational change of an enzyme, and from the assembly of a pore-forming toxin, each accompanied by measured data. The course exists to supply what a first reaction engineering course leaves as an assertion, namely where the Arrhenius prefactor comes from and why rates depend on the medium.

Inference from Noisy Data: Bayesian methods for experimentalists

Proposed graduate elective. Prerequisites: probability and programming in Python or MATLAB.

A single procedure taught until it is second nature: state a forward model, state a noise model, infer the parameters, and report what the data do and do not constrain. Maximum likelihood, Bayesian estimation, Markov chain Monte Carlo, hidden Markov models for time series, and model selection. Applications are drawn from photon arrival streams, from images limited by counting statistics, and from a remote-sensing problem in which the quantity of interest is smaller than a pixel. The course closes on simulation-based inference, for models that can be simulated but not written down.

Soft Interfaces: colloids, polymer brushes and membranes

Proposed graduate elective, open to senior undergraduates. Prerequisites: thermodynamics.

Intermolecular and surface forces; electrostatic and steric stabilisation of colloids; grafted polymer layers and the transition from mushroom to brush; supported lipid bilayers; surface passivation; the stability of nanoparticles in biological fluids. A demonstration that takes five minutes makes the theory visible, as a pinch of salt turns a gold colloid from red to blue while a polymer-coated sample beside it does not change. The subject matter carries directly into formulation in the pharmaceutical, food, paint and agrochemical industries, and into my own measurements on crowded membranes.

Laboratory development

The instrument I want to build for research, a single-molecule measurement that runs on an inexpensive camera rather than a photon-counting detector, has a teaching counterpart. I would like to add to an undergraduate laboratory a sequence in which students build a simple instrument, characterise it honestly, and then model what they have measured.

A camera module and a light-emitting diode make a colorimeter and a fluorimeter, which teach the Beer–Lambert law, the construction of a calibration curve, and the meaning of a limit of detection far more convincingly than a sealed commercial instrument. A microcontroller reading humidity and gas sensors and driving a relay turns process control from a block diagram into a loop that a student can tune badly and then tune well. A capacitive probe in a column of grain teaches dielectric mixing and transport in a porous medium, and produces a number that matters to anyone who has stored a harvest.

The approach scales to a large class, since the components are inexpensive enough for every group to build rather than watch. It is also the honest form of experimental teaching, because students meet calibration drift, a poor electrical ground, and a fit that refuses to converge, which is what measurement is actually like. Results accumulated across cohorts can be published as an open dataset, which gives students a first taste of authorship.

Research supervision

During my doctoral work and since, I have supervised two junior graduate students, two project assistants and seven summer interns, and I am presently designing a two-month master's project that moves from bench fluorimetry to single-molecule confocal measurement over eight weeks. Supervising across that range, from a student who has not held a pipette to one building an instrument, has persuaded me that structure matters more than encouragement.

A new student's first month is taught rather than left to osmosis: laser and chemical safety, programming and version control, and a scheme for organising data so that a result can be found again two years later. Each student has a written plan that records what they are to learn as well as what they are to produce, revised each term, which makes drift visible while it can still be corrected. I meet students individually every week, and expect writing from the first year, because a student who writes only at the end of a doctorate has been taught to dread it.

Analysis code is shared and read by someone else before a result is believed, which catches more errors than any amount of individual care. I also expect students to use computational and machine-learning tools deliberately, with verification and a record of provenance, since those tools are already in every student's work and in mine, and pretending otherwise teaches nothing.

Teaching in Indian institutions

I took my bachelor's degree at the National Institute of Technology, Durgapur, and my master's and doctorate at the Indian Institute of Science, so the classroom I am preparing for is one in which I have sat. Three practical matters follow.

Students arrive with markedly different preparation, particularly in programming and probability. Short bridge sessions in the opening weeks, offered to all and pressed on those who need them, cost very little and prevent the quiet loss of students who would otherwise have done well.

Language is a practical barrier and not a sentimental one. At institutions in the Hindi-speaking states I would conduct tutorials and problem sessions bilingually. I write technical material in Hindi already, for a bilingual biodiversity atlas I maintain outside the laboratory, and explaining a mechanism to a reader without the vocabulary for it has sharpened my own account of it more than once.

Summer projects are where many students first encounter research, and students from state universities and smaller colleges rarely get the opportunity. I intend to reserve a share of summer positions for them.
