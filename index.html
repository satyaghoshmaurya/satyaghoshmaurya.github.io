I watch single proteins change shape, and time the moment they do.

I am a chemical engineer and a postdoctoral researcher in the Gilad Haran Lab at the Weizmann Institute of Science. Proteins are the working machines of a cell, and they work by changing shape: an enzyme closes over the molecules it acts on, then opens again. A protein spends almost all its time resting in one shape or the other. The switch itself is over in a few millionths of a second.

About

That brief switch, called the transition path, is where the mechanism of the change is decided, and it is what I try to catch. Chemical engineering was built for the bulk: reactors, columns and control loops that handle moles of molecules and average the fluctuations away. I do the same engineering on one molecule. A protein changing shape is a chemical reaction; reaction engineering has long measured how often such reactions happen, and timing the switch itself shows how: how high and how rough the barrier is, and what friction the molecule feels on its way over. The friction is a transport problem, the barrier a thermodynamic one, and the stream of photons that reports them is process data. How each subject looks at this scale.

The difficulty is light. I follow a protein through two dyes attached to it, which glow green or red depending on how far apart they are (a method called single-molecule FRET). In an ordinary microscope a switch lasting a few microseconds gives off only a photon or two, too few to time it. So we make each molecule many times brighter with metal nanostructures, tiny holes in an aluminium film called zero-mode waveguides and gold nanorods, and read the stream of light one photon at a time with statistics built for exactly that.

I trained as a chemical engineer at NIT Durgapur and the Indian Institute of Science, Bangalore. My Ph.D. followed bacterial toxins assembling into pores on membranes, and proteins moving through crowded polymer layers on a membrane surface: nucleation and growth, and transport at an interface, watched one molecule at a time. That training shapes how I see everything above.

What my laboratory will do

Chemical engineering at the scale of one molecule, in four directions.

  • Reaction engineering of a single moleculeTime a protein’s change of shape while it happens, and compare it directly with computer simulations of the same molecule.
  • Transport in crowded mediaMeasure how much the crowded inside of a cell slows a protein down, and where the textbook idea of viscosity stops working.
  • Devices engineered to be madeTurn the light-concentrating nanostructures from one-off cleanroom pieces into chips that can be copied cheaply.
  • Frugal single-molecule scienceA low-cost microscope that follows single molecules, for college teaching laboratories and, with clinical partners, diagnostics.

Research

What I work on now at the Weizmann Institute, and what I did for my doctorate.

Timing how proteins change shape

How long does an enzyme take to switch between its open and closed shapes? The crossing lasts microseconds. Measuring it directly, rather than inferring it from how often it happens, is the core of my postdoctoral work.

Transition paths →

Making single molecules brighter

Holes in an aluminium film narrower than the wavelength of light, and gold nanorods, make a molecule shine many times brighter: enough photons to follow an event that lasts a few microseconds.

Reading the data one photon at a time

Each photon arrives with a time and a colour, and nothing else. Statistical methods built for this, such as the H2MM method developed in the Haran lab, recover the protein’s changes of shape straight from the photon stream. In my Ph.D. the same care went into tracking single molecules in microscope movies.

Awadh Biodiversity Atlas: community natural history Work in progress

An open-access citizen-science project, still in its early stage, to document the overlooked native flora, insects, birds and wetland life of rural eastern Uttar Pradesh, in the Basti and Ayodhya districts. Draft bilingual profiles for more than 680 species so far, with tools for telling look-alikes apart and learning them; field verification, photographs and dated sightings from the region are the next stage. Working toward bilingual field guides and rural school science programmes.
